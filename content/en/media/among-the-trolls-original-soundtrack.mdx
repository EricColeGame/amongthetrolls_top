## Why the Among the Trolls Original Soundtrack Deserves a Real Listen

Released on October 14, 2026 — the same day the game itself launched — the Among the Trolls Original Soundtrack spends its full runtime inside the head of one character, Anna, rather than describing the forest around her.

That single decision shapes everything about how the album sounds. It's the reason the score works as a standalone listen instead of a souvenir, and it's the reason a twelve-track album about a myth-soaked wilderness contains almost none of the instruments you'd expect.

If you're deciding whether it's worth your time or your money, the short answer is yes — provided you know what you're buying. The longer answer involves a composer, a featured vocalist, twelve tracks, and a very deliberate set of creative choices. Here's all of it.

## The Sound: Five Sources, Endlessly Reworked

The album is built from a small, almost stubbornly limited toolkit. Understanding it makes the record easier to hear.

| Source | Role in the Mix |
| --- | --- |
| Piano | The acoustic foundation — usually the first thing you hear clean |
| Cello | Low-end weight and a human, woody warmth |
| Strings | Sustain and swell; carries tension through long passages |
| Analog synth | The destabilizer — pitching, layering, and distortion |
| Voice | The most human element and, at times, the most processed |

Everything starts acoustic and real. Then it gets pitched, layered, and distorted until you genuinely can't tell what you're hearing anymore. That's not sloppiness; it's the entire thesis of the score. The ground is shifting under Anna as she returns to a place she knows and finds it wrong. The music does the same thing to your ear.

This is why headphones help. On laptop speakers, the processed layers blur into one texture. On headphones, you can hear individual sources being stretched and re-pitched in real time, which is where the album's craft lives.

## No Kantele, No Folk Imagery — and That's the Point

If you expected a troll-and-forest soundtrack to arrive with traditional instruments and folk melodies, this one deliberately goes the other way. There's no kantele here, and no attempt to quote folk imagery directly. Carmosino chose a modern, cinematic palette instead.

That's a harder trick than it sounds. The album still has to carry the weight of old myths without ever playing them straight. It does that through track titles like "Mielikki" and "Walkers of the Isles," which gesture at deep folklore, while the music underneath stays resolutely contemporary.

| What You Might Expect | What the Album Actually Delivers |
| --- | --- |
| Traditional folk instruments | Piano, cello, strings, analog synth, voice |
| Heroic, sweeping themes | Interior, first-person emotional states |
| A memorable melody per area | Slow builds, textures, and timbral shifts |
| A short bonus album | 12 tracks, roughly 40 minutes |

## How to Listen to the Among the Trolls Original Soundtrack

A few practical approaches make the album land harder:

1. **First pass, in order, no game running.** The sequencing is intentional, and shuffling breaks it.
2. **Second pass, vocal tracks as a set.** Play tracks 5, 8, and 12 back to back and you'll hear how Argirò's voice is used as a recurring thread rather than a one-off feature.
3. **Third pass, headphones, low volume.** The distortion work is subtle and rewards quiet attention.
4. **Background pass, while exploring or late at night.** This is where the album earns its runtime as atmosphere.
